Projects

Neurostimulation

Brain stimulation is both a tool for probing how the brain works and an increasingly important treatment for psychiatric and neurological disorders. We build computational models of how techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), theta burst stimulation, transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) and temporal interference stimulation act on neural circuits, from cellular mechanisms of plasticity (e.g. calcium-dependent plasticity and metaplasticity) up to whole-brain network responses measured with concurrent TMS-EEG. We combine these models with data from clinical rTMS and Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy trials in depression to identify biomarkers of treatment response, working towards personalized, model-guided stimulation protocols.

Models & Theory

Computational modelling is the common thread running through almost all of our work. We develop and apply whole-brain (connectome-based) neural mass models, corticothalamic and neural field models, and detailed cortical microcircuit models, together with the mathematical theory needed to understand their behaviour. A major focus is methodology: fitting models to individual subjects’ EEG, MEG and fMRI data using deep-learning-based parameter estimation tools such as WhoBPyT, assessing how much individual-level information these models capture, and making whole-brain modelling more reproducible and accessible through open-source software, tutorials and courses.

Network Structure & Dynamics

Brain activity unfolds on a physical network of white matter connections: the connectome. We study how this structure shapes and constrains large-scale brain dynamics, including how structural and functional connectivity relate to each other and to cognition, how stimulation and activity propagate through brain networks, how the geometry and eigenmodes of the cortex organize patterns of activity, and how network properties vary between individuals and across development. This work combines diffusion MRI tractography, resting-state fMRI, EEG/MEG and fNIRS with connectome-based models.

Oscillations & Rhythms

Rhythmic activity such as the alpha rhythm is one of the most prominent features of EEG and MEG recordings, yet how it is generated is still debated. We use corticothalamic and intracortical models to understand how brain rhythms arise, why they differ across brain regions, brain states (such as wake and sleep) and the lifespan, and how they interact with periodic brain stimulation through entrainment and resonance. We also study how rhythms relate to slower processes such as activity-dependent myelination and plasticity, and how they can serve as biomarkers in clinical populations.

Brain Damage

Damage to the brain from stroke, injury or neurological disease disrupts not only the tissue directly affected but also the wider networks it connects to. We study how focal lesions alter large-scale brain structure and dynamics, for example how secondary thalamic degeneration after stroke produces the abnormal slowing seen in the EEG, and how these changes relate to cognitive and language impairments. Using connectome-based models, we aim to predict the network consequences of a given pattern of damage, and to inform clinical interventions such as neurosurgical planning and stimulation-based rehabilitation.

Mental Health

Psychiatric conditions such as depression, psychosis and schizophrenia are increasingly understood as disorders of brain circuits and networks. We use computational models and neurophysiological measurements to link cellular and circuit-level changes, such as reduced cortical inhibition in depression, to measurable EEG and fMRI signatures, and to characterize differences in how people at risk of psychosis learn and update their beliefs. Much of this work is tied to clinical studies at CAMH, including brain stimulation treatment trials for depression and the Toronto Adolescent and Youth (TAY) Cohort Study, with the goal of developing biomarkers that can guide diagnosis and treatment.
